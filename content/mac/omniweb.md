# OmniWeb

[OmniWeb](https://www.omnigroup.com/more) is dead, and this page exists
because I used it for years and it deserves a place somewhere better
than an "Omni Labs" download table.

## What it was

A web browser from The Omni Group, born in the NeXTSTEP era of the
mid-nineties — which means it predates Internet Explorer's monopoly, Netscape's
collapse, and Safari itself. For years it was *the* way to browse the
web if you cared about being on a Mac that behaved like a Mac.

When Apple built Safari on WebKit, OmniWeb adopted the same engine — and
then kept innovating on top of it while everyone else fought over market
share. Its signature ideas are all things mainstream browsers either
adopted years later or still haven't matched:

- **Visual tabs** — tabs as live page thumbnails in a drawer, not
  anonymous text strips. Every "tab preview" feature shipped since is a
  re-implementation of something OmniWeb did two decades ago.
- **Workspaces** — named sets of windows and tabs you could save and
  restore as units. Browser companies are still shipping worse versions
  of this in 2026 and acting like it's new.
- **Per-site settings** — zoom, ad blocking, pop-up behavior, fake user
  agents, configured per website. Your browser today gives you a reader
  mode and calls it even.

## How it died

Not dramatically. Browsers became a war between three engine owners who
all sell advertising, and a small private company whose browser didn't
monetize attention couldn't outspend Google's Chromium budget. Development
wound down through the early 2010s; version 5 tops out at El Capitan;
the "OmniWeb 6 test builds" for modern macOS have sat on a staging URL
long enough to become a permanent address.

The [download page](https://www.omnigroup.com/more) still says "Better
browsing for everyone" above the words "no longer actively maintained,"
which is either heartbreaking or the most honest product page on the
internet.

## Verdict

Gone, but correct. OmniWeb's whole thesis — that a browser should be a
tool you configure instead of an engagement funnel you sit inside — lost
the market and won the argument. Every time Chrome shows you a sponsored
suggestion in your own address bar, remember that a NeXTSTEP shop from
Seattle had it right in 1995.
