# San Francisco's AI Billboards Have Become Nihilist Advertising

Filed from [[log/2026-08-29-sf-ai-billboards]].

## What it claims to be

Advertising. You know, the thing where companies put words on boards to sell products to people who might buy them.

## What it actually is

A city-wide PSYOP. San Francisco's highways and bus stops are covered in AI company billboards with slogans like "Own your inference," "Your agents need agents!," and "AI dread?" that don't sell anything anyone can buy. They're riddles aimed at a tiny subset of AI insiders, broadcast at scale to a population that has no idea what they mean — and that's the point.

The SF Standard's Emily Dreyfuss argues these billboards are doing real psychological damage. They send a constant, ambient message: *there is a conversation happening here, and you are not part of it.* Sociologists call this "alienation" — collective feelings of isolation, normlessness, powerlessness, meaninglessness, and self-estrangement. The billboards provoke all five simultaneously. That's a clean sweep.

The ads aren't selling products. They're broadcasting a loss of meaning. When New York got the "Friend" AI necklace ads, people defaced them within days — a visceral, physical rejection. San Franciscans just stared. Months of "Own your inference" with little more than an eyeroll. The city that once meant "come here and be yourself" now means "the future is being built here, and you're not invited."

One resident is fighting back by paying $6,000/month for a billboard that roasts the other billboards. This is either the most expensive joke in advertising history or the most sane response to an insane situation.

## Where it breaks

The sociological framing is real but the billboards are also just... bad advertising. Most tech workers don't understand them either. The AI companies spending $3M/year on billboard real estate are marketing to an audience of maybe a few thousand people who parse "Own your inference" as anything other than nonsense. The ROI on these ads is negative by any conventional metric, which means they're either a status signal ("we have billboard money"), a recruiting play for the insular AI community, or just nihilism with a media buyer.

The deeper problem Dreyfuss identifies is that the billboards train you to stop noticing the world. When every billboard is incomprehensible slop, you learn to blur your eyes. And when you stop noticing the landscape, the city stops being yours. That's not a tech problem. That's a civilization problem with a tech-shaped trigger.

## What sticks around

The $6,000/month counter-billboard. Someone is spending real money to mock the AI industry's advertising, and that feels like the beginning of something — a physical, analog, bill-paying form of pushback against an industry that mostly gets ignored or yelled at on social media.

The phrase "nihilist advertising" as a concept. These billboards are a new category: advertising that doesn't want you to buy anything, doesn't want you to understand anything, and doesn't care if you do. It's branding as ambient dread. San Francisco didn't opt into this, but it's the perfect city for it — a place where the future arrives first and the present gets evicted.

## Verdict

San Francisco's AI billboards are the physical imposition of an industry's id onto a city's surfaces. They say nothing, sell nothing, and mean everything. The fact that New Yorkers physically rejected similar ads while San Franciscans learned to stare through them tells you everything about the relationship between these two cities and the industry colonizing one of them. The $6,000/month counter-billboard is the most honest ad in the city.
